# Glossary for Your Personal Streaming Service Project

Organized by area, with how each term fits *your* project and where to learn more.

## Streaming Protocols

**HLS (HTTP Live Streaming)**
Apple's adaptive streaming protocol. Video is chopped into small segments (a few seconds each) listed in a playlist file (`.m3u8`). The player downloads segments in sequence and can switch quality on the fly.
- *Your project:* Required for iPhone/Safari — Apple devices strongly prefer (and on iOS, effectively require) HLS. This is why you can't pick just one protocol.
- *Learn more:* [Apple HLS overview](https://developer.apple.com/streaming/), [MDN: Live streaming web audio and video](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/Media/Guides/Streaming)

**DASH (Dynamic Adaptive Streaming over HTTP)**
The open-standard equivalent of HLS (also called MPEG-DASH). Same core idea — segmented video with a manifest — but codec-agnostic and not tied to Apple. Its manifest file is called an `.mpd`.
- *Your project:* Preferred for Android and web browsers. You'll likely package your content as *both* HLS and DASH to cover all devices.
- *Learn more:* [MDN DASH guide](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/Media/Guides/DASH_Adaptive_Streaming_for_HTML_5_Video), [DASH Industry Forum](https://dashif.org/)

**Adaptive Bitrate (ABR) streaming**
The technique both HLS and DASH use: you provide multiple quality versions (renditions) of the same video, and the player automatically picks the best one for the viewer's current bandwidth, switching as conditions change.
- *Your project:* This is what prevents constant buffering when your family watches on different/weaker connections. You create 1080p/720p/480p renditions so the player can adapt.
- *Learn more:* Covered in the MDN streaming guides above.

**ABR Algorithm**
The decision logic inside the player that answers, for every segment, "which rendition should I download next?" It balances quality against the risk of stalling. Three families:
- **Throughput-based (rate-based):** pick quality from measured recent bandwidth. Fast to react; can oscillate.
- **Buffer-based:** pick quality from how full the playback buffer is (e.g., the **BOLA** algorithm). Smoother; slower to ramp up.
- **Hybrid:** combine both. What most production players (including ExoPlayer's default) use.
- *Your project:* The "ABR algorithms" requirement is met not by *enabling* adaptation but by *observing, tuning, and demonstrating* it — customize ExoPlayer's `AdaptiveTrackSelection`, visualize switch decisions, and record adaptation under throttled network.

**Manifest / Playlist (`.m3u8` for HLS, `.mpd` for DASH)**
The text file that describes the stream: what renditions exist, where the segments are, their durations. The player reads this first, then fetches segments.
- *Your project:* These files get generated by your packaging step and stored in S3. The URL you hand to a player *is* the manifest URL.

**Segment (or chunk)**
The small pieces (typically 2-10 seconds) a video is split into for streaming.
- *Your project:* One movie becomes hundreds of segment files per rendition — relevant for S3 storage layout and why you want a CDN in front.

## Video Fundamentals

**Codec (Coder-Decoder)**
The algorithm that compresses/decompresses video or audio. Common video codecs: **H.264** (aka **AVC** — universal compatibility), **H.265** (aka **HEVC** — better compression, less universal support), **VP9**, **AV1**. Common audio codecs: **AAC** (universal) and **EAC3** (Enhanced AC-3 / Dolby Digital Plus — surround sound, common on Blu-ray/commercial streaming).
- *Your project:* Your DVD/Blu-ray rips are in some codec; you'll transcode video to AVC (safe, plays everywhere) and maybe HEVC (smaller files). For audio, handle **AAC** and pass through/handle **EAC3** tracks from your rips — demonstrating a multi-codec matrix. This directly satisfies your AVC/HEVC/AAC/EAC3 codecs requirement.
- *Learn more:* [MDN codecs guide](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/Media/Guides/Formats/Video_codecs)

## Captions & Subtitles

**CEA-608 / CEA-708**
Broadcast-style closed-caption standards embedded *in-band* (inside the video stream itself). **CEA-608** is the older "line 21" analog-era format (limited styling); **CEA-708** is the modern digital successor (richer styling, positioning, fonts).
- *Your project:* Satisfies the captions requirement. Get or inject 608/708 captions and render them in ExoPlayer with a toggle. ExoPlayer supports these natively.

**WebVTT (and SRT)**
*Sidecar* subtitle formats — separate text files referenced by the manifest, rather than embedded in the video. **WebVTT** is the web/HLS-native format; **SRT** is a common simple format.
- *Your project:* Know the distinction between **in-band** (CEA-608/708) and **sidecar** (WebVTT/SRT) captions — a likely interview talking point. You can package WebVTT alongside your HLS as an alternative caption path.

**Container / Format (MP4, MKV, TS)**
The file "wrapper" that holds the encoded video + audio + metadata together. Distinct from the codec inside it (an `.mp4` can contain H.264 or H.265).
- *Your project:* Rips are often `.mkv`; streaming segments are usually `.ts` (HLS) or fragmented `.mp4` (DASH). Knowing container ≠ codec avoids a common beginner confusion.

**Transcoding**
Converting video from one codec/format/bitrate to another. Includes creating your multiple ABR renditions.
- *Your project:* The heavy-lifting batch step — turning one big rip into multiple stream-ready renditions. Done with FFmpeg or AWS MediaConvert.

**Bitrate**
How much data per second of video (e.g., 5 Mbps). Higher = better quality + bigger files. Each ABR rendition targets a different bitrate.
- *Your project:* You pick a bitrate ladder (e.g., 1080p@5Mbps, 720p@2.8Mbps, 480p@1.4Mbps) when transcoding.

## DRM & Content Protection

**DRM (Digital Rights Management)**
Technology that encrypts content and controls who can decrypt/play it, via a license server that hands out decryption keys to authorized players.
- *Your project:* Optional/educational here since it's your own content — but it's the resume requirement. You'd encrypt content and integrate a license server so only authorized family devices can decrypt.
- *Learn more:* [MDN EME (Encrypted Media Extensions)](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/Encrypted_Media_Extensions_API)

**Widevine**
Google's DRM system, used on Android, Chrome, and most non-Apple platforms. Three security levels (L1/L2/L3).
- *Your project:* The DRM you'd tackle first (Android/web). Google publishes test streams and license servers to practice against.

**FairPlay**
Apple's DRM system, required for DRM-protected playback on iOS/Safari.
- *Your project:* The hardest DRM path (needs an Apple Developer account). Treat as optional/last.

**PlayReady**
Microsoft's DRM system, used on Windows, Edge, and Xbox.
- *Your project:* The third DRM ecosystem — the big three map to platforms: **Widevine** (Google/Android/Chrome), **FairPlay** (Apple), **PlayReady** (Microsoft). You only need to *demo Widevine*; know PlayReady conceptually and why multiple DRM systems exist (each platform mandates its own).

**AES-128 encryption (for HLS)**
A lighter form of protection: segments are encrypted with a key, and the player fetches the key from a URL you control. Not full DRM, but teaches content encryption.
- *Your project:* A good middle step between "signed URLs" and "full Widevine DRM."

**Signed URLs / Signed Cookies (CloudFront)**
Time-limited, cryptographically signed links that grant temporary access to content. Not encryption — access control. The content isn't scrambled, but only holders of a valid signed link can fetch it.
- *Your project:* Your realistic, easy-first access control — gate your family's content without full DRM.
- *Learn more:* [CloudFront signed URLs docs](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AmazonCloudFront/latest/DeveloperGuide/PrivateContent.html)

## Tools & Infrastructure

**FFmpeg**
The open-source Swiss-army knife for transcoding, packaging, and manipulating audio/video from the command line.
- *Your project:* Your primary transcoding/packaging tool if hand-rolling the pipeline (free, runs on your laptop).
- *Learn more:* [FFmpeg documentation](https://ffmpeg.org/documentation.html)

**Shaka Packager**
Google's open-source tool for packaging content into HLS/DASH and applying DRM encryption.
- *Your project:* Alternative/complement to FFmpeg for the packaging + DRM step.

**Shaka Player**
Google's open-source JavaScript player that plays DASH and HLS (with DRM) in browsers.
- *Your project:* Candidate for your laptop/web client.
- *Learn more:* [Shaka Player docs](https://shaka-player-demo.appspot.com/docs/api/tutorial-welcome.html)

**hls.js / dash.js**
JavaScript libraries that play HLS and DASH respectively in web browsers.
- *Your project:* Simpler web-player options for the laptop/iPhone-browser experience.

**ExoPlayer / Media3**
Google's official Android media player library (now part of "Media3"). Handles HLS, DASH, codecs, and DRM natively.
- *Your project:* The core of your Android app.
- *Learn more:* [Media3 ExoPlayer docs](https://developer.android.com/media/media3/exoplayer)

**S3 (Simple Storage Service)**
AWS object storage. Where your manifests and segments live.
- *Your project:* Origin storage for all packaged content.

**CloudFront**
AWS's CDN (Content Delivery Network) — caches/serves your S3 content efficiently and enforces signed-URL access control.
- *Your project:* Sits in front of S3 for efficient segment delivery and access gating.

**CDN (Content Delivery Network)**
A network of edge servers that deliver content from locations near the viewer. CloudFront is AWS's CDN.
- *Your project:* Why streaming (many small segment requests) stays fast.

**AWS MediaConvert / MediaPackage**
Managed AWS services for transcoding (MediaConvert) and packaging + DRM (MediaPackage) — the "let AWS do the FFmpeg work" option.
- *Your project:* The managed alternative to hand-rolling FFmpeg/Shaka. Learn concepts faster; costs money per use.

**Lambda / API Gateway / DynamoDB**
Serverless AWS building blocks — run code without servers (Lambda), expose it as an API (API Gateway), store data (DynamoDB).
- *Your project:* Candidate stack for your small catalog/auth backend.

## Android / App Architecture Terms

**Android SDK**
The toolkit (APIs, libraries, tools) for building Android apps.

**Activity**
A single screen with a UI. The fundamental Android UI entry point.
- *Your project:* Your app's screens (e.g., the player screen) are hosted in Activities.

**Fragment**
A reusable, modular piece of UI that lives inside an Activity. (Modern Compose apps often use fewer Fragments, but you should understand them.)

**Lifecycle**
The defined sequence of states a component (Activity/Fragment) passes through — created, started, resumed, paused, stopped, destroyed — as the user navigates and the system manages resources.
- *Your project:* Critical for the video player — you must pause playback and release the player at the right lifecycle moments, and resume correctly.
- *Learn more:* [Activity lifecycle docs](https://developer.android.com/guide/components/activities/activity-lifecycle)

**Jetpack Compose**
Android's modern declarative UI toolkit (you describe *what* the UI should look like for a given state, and it renders it). Replaces the older XML-layout approach.
- *Your project:* How you'll build your browse/catalog and player-control UIs.
- *Learn more:* [Android Basics with Compose course](https://developer.android.com/courses/android-basics-compose/course)

**Recomposition**
When Compose re-runs your UI code because the underlying state changed. Doing it too often causes performance problems (jank).
- *Your project:* Something you'll watch for with the Profiler, especially with per-second player updates.

**Custom View**
A UI component you draw yourself using the `Canvas`/`onDraw` APIs, when built-in components aren't enough.
- *Your project:* Custom playback controls (seek bar with buffered progress, quality selector).

**Animation**
Motion in the UI — transitions, fades, expansions.
- *Your project:* Fading player controls, thumbnail-to-player transitions.

**MVVM (Model-View-ViewModel)**
An architecture pattern separating UI (View) from state/logic (ViewModel) from data (Model). State is exposed as multiple observable properties.
- *Your project:* For your browse/catalog screen.

**MVI (Model-View-Intent)**
A stricter pattern where the whole screen is one immutable state object and every user action is an explicit "intent." Unidirectional data flow.
- *Your project:* A good fit for the player screen (complex interacting states).

**ViewModel**
An Android architecture component that holds and manages UI state, surviving configuration changes like rotation.
- *Your project:* Holds catalog data and player state.

**Thread / Main Thread / Background Thread**
The main (UI) thread handles rendering and must stay unblocked; heavy work (network, database, transcoding-status calls) goes on background threads.
- *Your project:* Network calls to your catalog API and player event handling must stay off the main thread.

**Coroutines**
Kotlin's mechanism for asynchronous/concurrent code — the modern way to do background work and manage threads on Android.
- *Your project:* How you'll do network calls and manage the timer/event streams.
- *Learn more:* [Kotlin coroutines on Android](https://developer.android.com/kotlin/coroutines)

**Flow**
Kotlin's reactive stream type — a sequence of values emitted over time that you can transform and combine. Built on coroutines.
- *Your project:* Satisfies your "reactive frameworks" requirement — wrap player events (buffering, position, errors) and catalog updates as Flows.
- *Learn more:* [Kotlin Flow docs](https://developer.android.com/kotlin/flow)

**RxJava**
An older, powerful reactive-streams library (the predecessor to Flow in most new Android code). Same "streams of events" concept, different API.
- *Your project:* Alternative to Flow for the reactive requirement; Flow is the modern default, but RxJava knowledge is still widely requested.

**Room**
Android's official local database library (a wrapper over SQLite).
- *Your project:* Cache the catalog/metadata locally so the app works smoothly offline and loads fast.
- *Learn more:* [Room codelab](https://developer.android.com/codelabs/basic-android-kotlin-compose-persisting-data-room)

**Hilt / Dependency Injection (DI)**
A pattern (and Google's library, Hilt) for supplying components their dependencies from outside rather than creating them internally — improves testability and decoupling.
- *Your project:* Wires together your ViewModels, repositories, and player — and supports the SOLID principles + testing requirements.
- *Learn more:* [Hilt docs](https://developer.android.com/training/dependency-injection/hilt-android)

**Android Profiler**
Android Studio's built-in tool for measuring CPU, memory, network, and rendering performance.
- *Your project:* Satisfies your "performance evaluation tools" requirement — hunt for player memory leaks, jank, and excessive recomposition.
- *Learn more:* [Android Studio Profiler docs](https://developer.android.com/studio/profile)

**Foreground Service**
An Android component for long-running work the user is aware of, with a persistent notification.
- *Your project:* Relevant if you support background audio playback (keep playing when the app isn't in front).

## Recommendations & Applied GenAI

**AWS Bedrock**
AWS's managed service for calling foundation models (LLMs) via API — pay per token.
- *Your project:* Powers explainable "For You" recommendations over your catalog and/or a natural-language "what should I watch?" search. Kept **server-side** (in your Lambda backend), never called from the client. Recovers Bedrock as a legitimately-placed skill on the media project.

**Collaborative Filtering**
The classic recommendation approach ("people who watched X also watched Y") based on user/item similarity — traditionally *not* an LLM.
- *Your project:* Know why you *didn't* use it — a small static catalog + single household + wanting natural-language explanations makes an LLM the pragmatic choice. Being able to justify this shows engineering judgment.

**RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation)**
Feeding an LLM your own data (retrieved from a store) so its answer is grounded in that data rather than its training.
- *Your project:* The pattern behind "recommend from *my* catalog" and NL search — you retrieve catalog + history, then have Bedrock reason over them.

**Hallucination**
When an LLM generates plausible-but-false output (e.g., recommending a title you don't own).
- *Your project:* Mitigated by instructing the model to use only the provided catalog **and validating returned title IDs against the real library** before display. Call this out as a design decision.

**Prompt / Token**
The input text you send an LLM (**prompt**); **tokens** are the chunks of text billed and processed (roughly ¾ of a word each).
- *Your project:* You build a prompt from history + catalog; token count drives Bedrock cost — so **cache** recommendations and regenerate only when history changes.



You don't need all of these before starting. Learn in this order:

1. **Android fundamentals** (Activity, lifecycle, Compose, ViewModel, coroutines, Room) — for the app itself.
2. **Streaming basics** (HLS, DASH, codec, container, transcoding, ABR, manifest, segment) — for your media pipeline spike.
3. **AWS delivery** (S3, CloudFront, signed URLs, CDN) — to serve content to your family.
4. **Player integration** (ExoPlayer/Media3, Shaka Player, hls.js) — to build the clients.
5. **Media depth** (ABR algorithm families, CEA-608/708 vs WebVTT captions, AVC/HEVC/AAC/EAC3 codec matrix) — the requirement-proving details.
6. **Content protection** (signed URLs → AES-128 → Widevine → FairPlay/PlayReady conceptually) — the DRM capstone, learned late.
7. **Applied GenAI** (Bedrock recommendations: RAG, prompts/tokens, hallucination mitigation) — optional final enhancement.
